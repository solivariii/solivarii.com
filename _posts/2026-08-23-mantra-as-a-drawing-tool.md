---
title: Mantra as a drawing tool
description: Repeating a mantra while drawing started as a way to stay focused. It became a way to measure lines, pace a day, and notice when the hand and the mind come apart.
---

Mantra practice, *japa*, is usually done seated, with a string of 108 beads moving through the fingers. One bead per repetition, one round, then another. I started saying a mantra while drawing for a much more practical reason: I kept losing my place.

## The rhythm becomes a ruler

A long yantra drawing involves hundreds of nearly identical gestures. Petal after petal, line after line. Somewhere around the fortieth petal the mind wanders, and the fortieth petal comes out a little different from the thirty-ninth.

With the mantra running quietly, each petal takes the same number of repetitions. The sound becomes a kind of metronome. When a petal takes two more repetitions than its neighbours, I look again, and usually find that something drifted.

## What changed

1. **Consistency.** The repeated forms in my drawings are noticeably more even.
2. **Pace.** I stopped rushing the boring parts. The mantra doesn't care whether the section is interesting.
3. **Honesty.** I can feel the moment my attention leaves the line, because the mantra stumbles at the same instant.
4. **Fatigue.** Strangely, long sessions are less tiring. The mind has one job instead of a dozen.

## A caution

In the tradition, a mantra is received from a teacher and treated with care. I don't think it is a productivity trick, and I would not recommend borrowing someone else's sacred syllables to finish a commission faster. What I am describing is the other direction: practice leaking into the studio, rather than the studio using practice.

> The line and the syllable both come from the breath. Neither should be hurried.
