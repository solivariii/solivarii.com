---
title: The grid under the thangka
description: Tibetan scroll painters work inside strict proportional systems. I expected the rules to feel like a cage. They turned out to be the opposite.
---

Before a Tibetan thangka painter draws a single deity, there is a grid. Lines are snapped across the primed cloth, and the figure is measured out in units: so many for the face, so many for the torso, so many from the knee to the sole. The proportions differ for a peaceful buddha, a goddess, a wrathful protector. They are written down, taught, and followed.

## The rules are the point

Coming from art school, my instinct was to resist this. Wasn't the artist supposed to invent? But a thangka is not a self-portrait of the painter's taste. It is a support for visualisation. A practitioner will spend years building that exact figure in the mind's eye, and the painting has to match the text so the two can meet.

Once that sank in, the grid stopped looking like a cage. It is a promise: everyone who looks at this figure will find the same body in the same place.

## What the grid leaves open

Plenty, as it happens:

- The **landscape** around the figures, the clouds, rocks, water and flowers, is where painters have always shown their hand.
- **Line quality** cannot be measured. Two painters can follow the same grid and produce two completely different presences.
- **Colour transitions**, the soft shading built up in dozens of thin layers, belong to the painter alone.
- **Gold work**, burnished and then incised with fine patterns, is a whole art inside the art.

## What I took from it

I now begin most large paintings with a measured underdrawing, even when nothing in the final image looks geometric. The grid disappears under the paint, but the figure stays where it was put. And because I am no longer deciding where everything goes, there is more attention left for how it goes there.

That is the strange gift of a strict form. It takes away the decisions that never mattered, and gives you back the ones that do.
